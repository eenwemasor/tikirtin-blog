Nobody tells you how hard event organizing actually is until you're already in it.

The planning spreadsheets. The vendor negotiations. The sponsors who confirm and then go quiet for two weeks. The guest list that looked solid until three days before the event. The moment the sound system stops working during the opening keynote and every single person in the room turns to look at you.

Event organizing in Nigeria is a craft - and like any craft, the real lessons come from doing it, getting it wrong, and doing it again.

We spoke with event organizers from across Nigeria - running everything from corporate summits and brand activations to music festivals and professional networking events - about what they find genuinely hard, what they've learned the painful way, and what they think is coming next for the industry.

Here's what they had to say.


## "The hardest part isn't planning the event. It's getting people to actually show up."

**Tunde, Corporate Events Manager, Lagos**

*Tunde has been organizing B2B conferences and executive summits for seven years. He estimates he's managed over 40 events.*

"Everyone assumes the hard part is logistics - the venue, the catering, the AV. Those things are stressful, but they're manageable. You plan them, you execute, you solve problems as they come.

The thing that genuinely keeps me up at night is attendance. You can sell 500 tickets and have 280 people walk through the door. That's happened to me. It's humiliating when you've set up a room for 500.

What I've learned is that selling a ticket and getting someone to show up are two completely different jobs. The ticket is the beginning. Then you have to keep that person warm, keep them excited, keep reminding them that this event is worth their Friday evening or their Saturday morning. Most organizers stop communicating after the confirmation email goes out. That's the mistake.

Now I run a full reminder sequence - one week out, 48 hours, morning of. I share speaker content on social media in the lead-up. I get attendees engaged before they even arrive. My show-up rates are completely different now."


## "I used to be terrified of technology. Now I don't know how I ran events without it."

**Amaka, Founder of a Lagos-based Events Company**

*Amaka started organizing community events five years ago, grew into corporate activations, and now runs a team of six.*

"When I first started, everything was manual. Bank transfers, screenshots, Google Sheets. I thought that's just how it worked. I thought the tech was for bigger organizers with bigger budgets - not for someone running events of 100 or 200 people.

Then I did an event where three people showed up at the gate claiming they'd paid and their names weren't on my list. Two of them were genuinely confused - the third, I'm still not sure. But I had no way to verify anything in real time. It was chaos. People were upset. I was upset. It was embarrassing.

After that, I moved everything digital. Ticketing platform, QR codes at the gate, the whole thing. My very next event was smoother than anything I'd done before. Check-in was fast. I knew exactly who had paid. No arguments, no confusion. I kept thinking - why did I wait so long?

The perception that digital tools are only for big events is completely wrong. If anything, smaller organizers need them more, because you usually don't have a large team to absorb the chaos when things go wrong manually."


## "Nigerian audiences have changed dramatically. They expect more now."

**Chidi, Music and Entertainment Promoter, Abuja**

*Chidi has promoted concerts, music festivals, and artist showcases for a decade, working with both emerging and established acts.*

"Ten years ago, if the show started two hours late, people would grumble but they'd stay. That was just how it was. Now? If you're not running on time, if the sound isn't right, if the experience feels disorganized - people will say it publicly and loudly. On Twitter, on Instagram, in group chats. The reputational risk is real.

The bar has gone up everywhere. Attendees at Lagos concerts have been to international events, they've watched livestreams of world-class productions, they've seen what's possible. They bring those expectations to every event they attend here.

For me, that's actually exciting. It means the organizers who put in the work - who manage their events properly, who communicate clearly, who invest in the experience - stand out more than ever. The gap between a well-run event and a poorly-run one is obvious now, and audiences vote with their wallets.

What I've learned is that every touchpoint matters. Not just the show itself - how people buy tickets, how they receive confirmation, how they check in, how they're treated at the gate. All of it is part of the experience."


## "My biggest lesson: know your numbers before you spend a single naira."

**Ngozi, Conference Organizer and Consultant, Lagos**

*Ngozi has organized over 25 professional conferences and now consults with other organizers on event strategy and financial planning.*

"I made a catastrophic financial mistake on my third event. I booked the venue, confirmed the caterer, paid deposits on everything - before my tickets were even on sale. I was confident it would sell. It didn't sell the way I expected.

I ended up personally covering a significant shortfall. It took me almost a year to recover financially.

After that, I became obsessive about numbers. I don't spend on variable costs until I've hit a certain ticket sales threshold. I have a hard rule: no major vendor commitments until Early Bird closes and I can see the trajectory.

The other thing nobody talks about is fees. If you're selling tickets and you don't account for processing fees, VAT, service charges - those percentages add up fast and they come out of your payout, not the ticket price. I've talked to organizers who were genuinely shocked when their payout was lower than expected because they hadn't modeled the fees properly.

Now I use a platform that shows me the full cost breakdown before I publish any ticket. I know exactly what my payout will be per tier, so my financial model is based on reality, not assumptions."


## "Sponsors are not charity. They need ROI. Start treating them that way."

**Emeka, Tech and Innovation Events Organizer, Lagos**

*Emeka runs an annual technology conference that has grown from 150 to over 800 attendees over four years.*

"Early in my career, I used to approach sponsors with essentially a wish list. 'Here's our event, here's what we'd like you to contribute, here's a logo placement on the banner.' And I'd wonder why so many said no.

Sponsors are businesses. They need to justify every naira to their marketing or leadership team. When you approach them without a clear ROI story, you're asking them to take a leap of faith. Most won't.

What changed for me was starting to collect real data on my attendees. Job titles, company sizes, industries, seniority levels. Once I could walk into a sponsorship conversation and say 'Last year, 68% of our attendees were senior decision-makers in financial services and tech, with an average company size of 50+ employees' - the conversation completely changed. That's not a vague audience. That's a target market.

My advice to any organizer trying to grow their sponsorship revenue: your attendee data is your pitch deck. Start collecting it properly from day one."


## "The events industry in Nigeria is about to go through a serious professionalization phase."

**Folake, Events Strategist and Industry Observer, Lagos**

*Folake has worked in events for 15 years, spanning hospitality, entertainment, and B2B, and now advises organizations on their event strategies.*

"I've watched this industry evolve enormously. When I started, 'event organizer' wasn't something people said with respect. It was seen as something you did on the side, not a real profession. That has genuinely changed.

What's driving it is a combination of things. The economy has made events a serious revenue channel for businesses - they're not just nice-to-haves anymore, they're marketing, they're sales, they're community building. So businesses are taking them more seriously, which means they're demanding more professionalism from organizers.

At the same time, the tools have gotten much better. Five years ago, if you wanted to run a properly managed digital ticketing operation in Nigeria, your options were limited. Now there are platforms built specifically for this market - with local payment support, proper check-in tools, analytics - that make the infrastructure side accessible to any organizer at any scale.

What's coming next? I think you'll see a continued bifurcation. Organizers who invest in their craft, use proper tools, and treat events as a professional discipline will build brands that grow year on year. Those who don't will find it increasingly hard to compete as audience expectations keep rising.

The exciting part is that the ceiling has never been higher. Nigerian events - properly run, properly marketed - can compete with the best in the world. We're already seeing it."


## What Every Organizer In This Conversation Agreed On

Different backgrounds, different event types, different scales - but some lessons came up again and again:

**Communication doesn't end when the ticket is sold.** Every organizer who had improved their show-up rates pointed to the same thing: staying in consistent contact with their audience from purchase to event day.

**Data is not optional anymore.** Whether it's attendee demographics for sponsor pitches or financial models based on real fee structures, the organizers who operate with real numbers make better decisions.

**The audience has changed.** Nigerian event-goers are more discerning, more vocal, and more likely to share their experience publicly - good or bad. The standard for what "good" looks like has risen sharply, and it will keep rising.

**Technology is an equalizer.** Access to professional-grade event management tools is no longer limited to large agencies or well-funded productions. An independent organizer with the right platform can run an event that feels just as polished as a corporate production.

**Every event is a data point for the next one.** The organizers who grow aren't just running events - they're studying them. Show-up rates, sales trajectories, which tiers sold fastest, which channels drove the most buyers. Every event teaches you something. The question is whether you're paying attention.
